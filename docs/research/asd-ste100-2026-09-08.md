## What it is and its purpose

ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English, "STE") is a controlled natural language: a standard that
restricts English vocabulary and grammar for writing technical documentation. Verbatim from the
standard's own general introduction:

> "The purpose of STE is to tell technical writers how to write technical texts in a clear, simple,
> and unambiguous manner that readers throughout the world will find easy to understand. STE is not
> a treatise on the English language or technical writing."
(ASD-STE100 Issue 9, General introduction, p. ii)

It is not a full style guide — it explicitly does not regulate abbreviations, text formatting, or
units of measurement, and is meant to be used together with other applicable specifications, style
guides, and directives (same page).

## History and origin

- First released **1986-02-15** as the *AECMA Simplified English Guide* (PSC-85-16598), produced by
  the European Association of Aerospace Industries (AECMA) with support from the Aerospace Industries
  Association of America (AIA), at the request of European airlines — to make aircraft maintenance
  documentation understandable to readers without native-level English. [www.asd-ste100.org/about_STE.html]
- The originating working group (SEWG) dates to **1983**; it was renamed STEMG in 2004 when AECMA's
  successor organizations formed ASD. [www.asd-ste100.org/ASD_STEMG.html] The standard's own
  acknowledgments page names the STEMG's founding chairpersons: René Van Dijk (1983–1987), Wally
  Grindrod (1987–1991), Wilfried Jacobs (1991–1994), Kathleen Barthe (1994–2007). (Issue 9, General
  introduction, p. vii)
- Full official release history, verbatim from the standard's own "History of ASD-STE100" table
  (Issue 9, General introduction, p. iv) — **17 releases** in total:

  | Date | Identification | Scope |

## Structure: Part 1 (writing rules) and Part 2 (dictionary)

Confirmed from the standard's own "Guide to the writing rules" section and table of contents
(Issue 9, General introduction p. vii; Part 1 section-summary pages):

**Part 1 — Writing rules**: "9 sections that contain 53 rules" (verbatim, p. vii). Sections:

1. Words (rules 1.1–1.14) — which words you may use, approved meanings, technical nouns (22
   categories, e.g. official parts information, tools, materials, law/regulations, colors — the last
   two categories were added in Issue 9) and technical verbs (4 categories: manufacturing processes,
   computer processes, subject-field instructions, law/regulations), American-English spelling.
2. Multi-word nouns (2.1–2.2) — cap technical nouns at **3 words**, verbatim (rule 2.1): *"Write
   multi-word nouns of no more than three words."*
3. Verbs (3.1–3.7)
4. Sentences (4.1–4.5)
5. Procedural writing (5.1–5.5)
6. Descriptive writing (6.1–6.6)
7. Safety instructions (7.1–7.3)
8. Punctuation and word count (8.1–8.7)
9. Writing practices (9.1–9.4), plus 8 "General recommendations" (GR-1 to GR-8; GR-7 "Inclusive
   language" and GR-8 "Possessive form" are new in Issue 9)

**Part 2 — Dictionary**: an approved-word list. Per the standard's own general introduction: "each
word has only one meaning and is approved with only one part of speech" — the "one word, one
meaning" rule (Issue 9, General introduction, p. ii, verbatim). Where standard English has synonyms,
STE keeps exactly one (e.g. "start," not "begin/commence/initiate/originate"). Approved meanings and
spelling follow American English and Merriam-Webster's dictionary. Each entry also gives the word's
approved grammatical forms (e.g. REMOVE / REMOVES / REMOVED / REMOVED; SLOW / SLOWER / SLOWEST).
Secondary reporting puts the dictionary at **~900 approved words** plus ~1,200 unapproved words with
suggested alternatives [multiple secondary sources, e.g. hyperste.ai, techscribe.co.uk] — I could not
independently re-derive that exact count from the PDF pages I read (a full word-by-word count would
require reading the entire Part 2 word list), so treat "~900 / ~1,200" as **secondary, unverified by
me directly** even though it's a widely repeated figure.

Company- or subject-field-specific technical nouns and technical verbs are deliberately *not* in the
dictionary — writers pull those from their own company glossary/terminology database, following the
category rules above.

## Notable rule specifics (verified directly from Issue 9 text)

- Rule 1.9: when no approved technical noun exists, pick one that's short (**not more than three
  words**) and easy to understand.
- Rule 1.14: use American English spelling per the dictionary, unless another official directive says
  otherwise.
- Rule 1.7/1.13: don't use a technical noun as a verb, or a technical verb as a noun.
- Rule 1.11: never use two different technical nouns for the same item within one document.
- "STE was developed for technical documentation only... but the rules and controlled dictionary can
  help oral communication" and can aid machine translation / LLM translation of technical text,
  per the standard's own FAQ section in the general introduction (p. iii).

## Governance: STEMG

The **ASD Simplified Technical English Maintenance Group (STEMG)** develops, produces, publishes,
maintains, and manages ASD-STE100 (Issue 9, General introduction, p. iv, verbatim). It reports to the
ASD Services Commission. [www.asd-ste100.org/ASD_STEMG.html] It draws experts from ASD member
countries plus international, non-ASD "partner" countries, and is supported by national/multi-country
**STE Support Teams (STEST)**. The STEMG meets twice a year and has held 90+ working meetings across
13 countries; it maintains a public change-request process (`stemg@asd-ste100.org`, a "change form"
reproduced in the standard itself, Issue 9 p. vi) for anyone to propose rule or dictionary changes.

Membership breakdown (11 ASD-country members incl. Italy, Germany, France, Spain, UK, Sweden; 8
non-ASD partner countries incl. USA, Japan, Australia) came from a summarized fetch of
`www.asd-ste100.org/ASD_STEMG.html` rather than text I read verbatim — **flagged as
secondary/unverified-by-me** even though the source page itself is official.

  to all writers and users through the STEMG website www.asd-ste100.org or through the ASD
  www.asd-europe.org and ASD-STAN websites www.asd-stan.org. Unauthorized distribution of ASD-STE100,
  direct or through different websites or portals, is strictly prohibited without written permission
  from the STEMG." (Issue 9, General introduction, p. iv)
- However, the standard's own **copyright page** ("Special usage rights") grants irrevocable,
  royalty-free use/reproduction/publication rights only to eight named categories: ASD national
  associations and their member companies; AIA and AIAC members; ICCAIA members; customers of
  companies in those three categories; Ministries of Defense of ASD/AIA/AIAC member countries;
  Airlines for America (A4A); airworthiness authorities; and universities/research institutes (for
  educational use). (Issue 9, copyright notices, p. i) This is a narrower, more precise statement than
  the "free to everyone" framing on the marketing/FAQ pages — worth flagging: the two statements on
  the official site aren't word-for-word reconciled, and I'm reporting both rather than picking one.
- Practically: the current PDF is downloadable after filling in a request form on the STEMG downloads
  page (collected "to help understand the use of STE") — distribution format is PDF only.

## Relationship to other standards

- The standard's own reference-documents list (Issue 9, General introduction, p. viii) cites: ISO
  1087:2019 (terminology work vocabulary), ISO 704:2022 (terminology work principles), ISO
  3864 (safety colours/signs), ISO 45001:2018, plus non-ISO style references (Merriam-Webster,
  Chicago Manual of Style, Gregg Reference Manual, Kirkman's *Punctuation Matters*, Swan's *Practical
  English Usage*). **I did not find ISO 24620-4:2023 in this list** — an earlier fetch of a secondary
  summary of `about_STE.html` claimed STE is "referenced in ISO 24620-4:2023"; I could not confirm
  this against the primary document's own reference list, so **flag it as unverified / possibly
  incorrect**.
- **S1000D**: the official FAQ states STE's role is "to support technical writing worldwide whenever
  there is a requirement" and describes it as referenced in connection with S1000D for land/sea
  vehicle documentation [www.asd-ste100.org/STE_faq.html, summarized fetch]. The mechanical detail —
  that S1000D governs content structure (data modules, CSDB, BREX) while STE governs the language
  inside that structure, and that S1000D programs typically mandate STE via project business rules
  rather than S1000D itself requiring it — is **[secondary]**, drawn from S1000D-focused industry
  sites (techwriter.ai, xignal-s1000d.com, an S1000D explainer on Medium), not from the ASD-STE100
  standard's own text or from S1000D's own specification, which I did not fetch.
- Aviation-authority mandates (EASA, FAA, CAAC referencing/requiring STE) are **[secondary]**, from a
  summarized fetch of `about_STE.html`; not independently confirmed against an EASA/FAA primary source.
